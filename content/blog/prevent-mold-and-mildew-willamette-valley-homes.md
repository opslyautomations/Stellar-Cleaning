The short answer to how to prevent mildew in your house is moisture control: keep indoor humidity under control, vent steam from bathrooms and kitchens, wipe up condensation before it sits, and clean damp-prone surfaces on a regular schedule. Mildew needs moisture to grow, so take the moisture away and it has nothing to work with. Small spots on shower caulk or a window sill are normal upkeep. Larger growth, or mold that keeps coming back, is a job for a mold remediation professional.

## Why valley homes fight mildew every winter

Willamette Valley winters are mild, gray and wet. The National Weather Service's [Climate of Eugene summary](https://www.weather.gov/media/pqr/climate/ClimateBookEugene/pg1.pdf) notes that nearly 90 percent of the area's annual rainfall falls between early October and mid-May. Through those months, houses stay closed up, laundry gets dried inside, and windows stay shut.

Inside, everyday life adds more moisture: showers, cooking, boiling pasta, running the dishwasher, even breathing. The Oregon Health Authority points out that when indoor air is warmer than outdoor air, moisture collects on cold surfaces like single-pane windows, uninsulated walls and pipes. That is the sweating window you see on a January morning in Corvallis or Eugene, and the black specks that show up on the sill a few weeks later.

Older rentals near campus, homes along the river in Springfield, and houses tucked under big trees in Lebanon all have their own versions of the same problem: damp air with nowhere to go.

## Mildew vs mold: what you are looking at

Mildew is the everyday name people use for mold growing on the surface of something. It usually looks gray, white or black and powdery, and shows up on shower grout, caulk, window sills, and the back of curtains. It tends to wipe off a hard, non-porous surface.

Mold that goes deeper, into drywall, wood, carpet backing or ceiling tiles, is a different problem. The EPA notes that porous materials like ceiling tiles and carpet may need to be thrown away if they become moldy, and that you should never paint or caulk over mold. A musty smell with no visible source can mean growth inside a wall or under a floor.

## Know your humidity target

The single best number to know is your indoor relative humidity. A small digital hygrometer costs little and tells you right away whether your house is too damp.

- The EPA's [mold and moisture guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent.
- The [Oregon Health Authority's mold page](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/HEALTHYENVIRONMENTS/HEALTHYNEIGHBORHOODS/TOXICSUBSTANCES/Pages/mold.aspx) recommends 30 to 60 percent, using dehumidifiers and exhaust fans to bring it down.

If your readings sit above 60 percent most of the winter, a dehumidifier in the dampest room, often a basement, laundry room or bedroom with an exterior wall, makes a real difference. Empty it or set it to drain.

## How to prevent mildew in your house, room by room

### Bathrooms

1. **Run the exhaust fan during every shower** and for a while after. The EPA recommends running the bathroom fan or opening a window when showering.
2. **Squeegee or towel off shower walls and glass** after use.
3. **Leave the shower curtain spread open** so it dries, and wash it regularly.
4. **Hang towels to dry fully** rather than in a heap on the floor.
5. **Clean grout and caulk weekly.** Mildew that gets caught early wipes away. Mildew left for months stains.
6. **Check the fan actually works.** Hold a square of toilet paper up to it. If it does not pull, it is not venting.

### Kitchen and laundry

- Use the range hood or open a window while cooking and running the dishwasher.
- Vent the clothes dryer outdoors and keep the lint path clear.
- Leave the front-loading washer door and detergent drawer open between loads so the gasket can dry.
- Wipe under the sink regularly and look for slow leaks.

### Windows and exterior walls

- Wipe condensation off glass and sills each morning in the coldest months.
- Open blinds and curtains during the day so air reaches the glass.
- Keep furniture a few inches off exterior walls so air can circulate behind it.
- Clean window tracks, where water pools and grime gives mildew something to feed on.

### The whole house

- Fix leaks fast. The EPA notes that if wet materials are dried within 24 to 48 hours of a leak or spill, in most cases mold will not grow.
- Keep gutters and downspouts clear so water drains away from the foundation.
- Dry laundry in a vented dryer rather than on racks indoors when you can.
- Open windows briefly on dry winter days to swap out damp air.

## When to call a professional, and who to call

The EPA's rule of thumb is that if the moldy area is less than about 10 square feet, roughly a 3-foot by 3-foot patch, you can usually handle it yourself. Larger areas, mold caused by sewage or contaminated water, or growth inside walls call for an experienced mold remediation professional, and suspected mold in the heating and cooling system calls for a duct and HVAC specialist. If anyone in the home has health concerns, talk to a health care provider.

The Oregon Health Authority also notes that testing for mold is generally not helpful, because there are no standards for safe levels. If you can see it or smell it, you have a moisture problem to fix.

If you rent, document the problem with photos and contact your landlord in writing. Check the current Oregon landlord-tenant law or talk to a tenant organization about what applies to your situation.

## Frequently asked questions

### What humidity level prevents mildew in a house?

Keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent, is the EPA's recommendation for preventing mold and mildew. The Oregon Health Authority gives a range of 30 to 60 percent. A small hygrometer shows your current level, and a dehumidifier, exhaust fans and good ventilation bring it down if your readings run high through the winter.

### Why do my windows sweat every morning in winter?

Your windows sweat because warm, moist indoor air hits glass that has been chilled by the cold outdoor air, and the moisture condenses. The Oregon Health Authority notes this happens on cold surfaces like single-pane windows and uninsulated walls. Lower indoor humidity, run exhaust fans, open blinds during the day and wipe sills dry each morning to keep mildew from growing.

### How much mold can I clean up myself?

The EPA says you can usually clean up a moldy area yourself if it is less than about 10 square feet, roughly a 3-foot by 3-foot patch. Larger areas, mold caused by contaminated water, mold in heating and cooling systems, or growth inside walls should be handled by an experienced remediation professional. Fix the moisture source either way, or it will come back.

### Should I test my house for mold?

Usually no. The Oregon Health Authority says mold testing is generally not helpful because there are no standards for safe levels of indoor mold, and a test cannot tell you whether the amount found will affect your health. If you can see mold or smell a musty odor, you already know there is a moisture problem to find and fix.
