Good medical office cleaning splits a clinic into two zones: non-clinical spaces like waiting rooms, restrooms, hallways and offices, which a trained contract crew cleans after hours, and clinical surfaces that your own staff disinfect between patients. If you are comparing medical office cleaning Salem providers, or looking for the same thing in Corvallis or Lebanon, the first question to settle is where that line sits, and to put it in writing.

## How Salem clinics can plan medical office cleaning

Most confusion comes from assuming the cleaning company handles everything. It should not.

The CDC's dental infection control guidance draws a useful line that applies well beyond dentistry. It separates [clinical contact surfaces from housekeeping surfaces](https://www.cdc.gov/dental-infection-control/hcp/dental-ipc-faqs/cleaning-disinfecting-environmental-surface.html). Clinical contact surfaces, such as light handles, bracket trays, unit switches and computer equipment in the treatment area, get spray and spatter during procedures and are barrier-protected or disinfected between patients. Housekeeping surfaces, such as floors, walls and sinks, do not touch patients or instruments and carry a lower risk.

In practice, that gives you a workable division:

- **Your clinical staff** handle exam tables, treatment chairs, instruments, clinical equipment and anything that touches a patient between appointments.
- **A contract cleaning crew** handles floors, waiting rooms, restrooms, front desks, offices, break rooms, hallways, glass, ordinary trash and restocking of paper goods and soap.
- **Licensed specialists** handle regulated medical waste, sharps and any biohazard cleanup.

Write that split into the scope of work so nothing gets cleaned twice or skipped.

## Waiting rooms and restrooms set the impression

Patients judge a practice by what they can see, and waiting rooms and restrooms take more hand contact than almost anywhere else in the building.

A nightly checklist for the non-clinical side of a clinic usually covers:

1. Door handles, push plates, light switches and elevator buttons
2. Check-in counters, clipboards, pens and payment terminals (exteriors only, per your device policy)
3. Chair arms and seat surfaces in the waiting area
4. Restroom fixtures, grab bars, dispensers, partitions and floors
5. Drinking fountains and break-room appliance handles
6. Trash and recycling, with liners replaced
7. Hard floors mopped and carpets vacuumed
8. Entry glass and interior partitions

High-touch points get disinfected, not just wiped. That distinction matters, and it brings us to the product on the cloth.

## Disinfectants, EPA registration and dwell time

A disinfectant only works the way its label says it works. Every product sold as a disinfectant in the US carries an EPA registration number, and the label lists which germs it is registered against and how long the surface has to stay wet to get that result. That wait is called contact time, or dwell time.

EPA's [six steps for safe and effective disinfectant use](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2020-04/documents/disinfectants-onepager.pdf) sum it up: confirm the product is EPA-registered, read the directions for use sites and surface types, pre-clean visibly dirty surfaces, keep the surface wet for the full contact time, wear gloves, and store products closed and out of reach.

The common failure is spray-and-wipe: a surface wiped dry before the label's contact time has been cleaned, not disinfected. A crew working after hours in an empty clinic has time to do it properly.

Many practices specify the exact disinfectant they want used, which is reasonable. We bring eco-friendly products as standard on our [commercial cleaning](/services/commercial-cleaning) accounts, and where a facility's compliance requires a specific product, we use that product.

## The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard, in plain terms

The federal [Bloodborne Pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1030), applies to employees who can reasonably be expected to contact blood or other potentially infectious materials as part of their job. In Oregon, it is enforced by Oregon OSHA, which adopts the federal standard by reference and adds some Oregon-specific requirements.

A few points matter for how you set up cleaning:

- The employer must keep the worksite clean and sanitary and must set a **written schedule for cleaning and decontamination**, based on the location, the type of surface, the amount of contamination and the procedures performed there.
- Equipment and surfaces must be cleaned and decontaminated after contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials.
- Each employer is responsible for training its own employees who have occupational exposure.

The simplest arrangement for a medical or dental office is to scope the contract crew out of blood exposure entirely. Blood spills in clinical areas are handled by your trained staff under your exposure control plan, and the cleaning crew works the housekeeping surfaces. This is a summary, not legal advice. Check the current standard and talk to your compliance officer or Oregon OSHA if you are unsure how it applies to your practice.

## Medical waste and biohazards go to licensed specialists

Oregon regulates infectious waste under its own statutes. The Oregon Health Authority's [infectious waste FAQ](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/DISEASESCONDITIONS/COMMUNICABLEDISEASE/Pages/InfectiousWasteFAQ.aspx) lists four categories: pathological waste, biological waste such as blood and body fluids, cultures and stocks, and sharps. It names the Oregon Health Authority, the Department of Environmental Quality and the Department of Transportation as regulators.

None of that belongs with a general cleaning contractor. When you scope medical office cleaning, Salem and valley practices alike should leave sharps containers, red bags and other regulated waste to clinical staff and a licensed medical waste hauler. Biohazard cleanup after a serious incident is specialist work too. A general cleaning company, including ours, does not offer it. A contract crew should leave those containers alone and tell you when something looks wrong.

## Dental offices have their own rhythm

Dental practices follow the same split, with a few differences worth noting in the scope:

- **Operatories.** The chair, light, tray and unit are clinical contact surfaces your team handles between patients. The crew cleans the floor, walls, sinks where agreed, and trash.
- **Sterilization rooms and labs.** Many practices keep these off-limits to cleaning crews or allow entry only under specific rules. Write that down.
- **Aerosol and fine dust.** Dental procedures settle fine material on horizontal surfaces, so ledges, vents and baseboards in treatment areas need regular detail work.
- **Front desk and waiting room.** Same high-touch list as any clinic, with extra attention to anything patients handle.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a cleaning company disinfect exam rooms between patients?

No, between-patient disinfection of exam tables, chairs and clinical equipment is your clinical staff's job, because it happens during the day as patients cycle through. A contract crew cleans exam room floors, walls, sinks where agreed, trash and touch points such as door handles and light switches, usually after hours. Spelling out that split in the scope of work prevents gaps and duplicated effort.

### Can a general cleaning crew remove sharps containers or red-bag waste?

No, regulated medical waste should be handled by your trained staff and collected by a licensed medical waste hauler, not a general cleaning crew. Oregon treats sharps, blood and body fluids, pathological waste and cultures as infectious waste with specific handling rules. A good contract crew leaves those containers untouched and lets you know if a container looks full, damaged or out of place.

### What is disinfectant dwell time and why does it matter?

Dwell time, also called contact time, is how long a disinfectant must stay wet on a surface to kill the germs listed on its label. If a surface is wiped dry early, it has been cleaned but not disinfected. The label states the time, and it varies by product and germ, so crews should follow the label for each product rather than a general habit.

### How often should a medical office be professionally cleaned?

Most medical and dental offices need cleaning every day they see patients, because waiting rooms, restrooms and touch points get heavy daily use. Smaller practices with limited hours sometimes run three nights a week with daytime touch-ups by staff. The right frequency depends on patient volume, hours and the size of the space, so it is best set during a walkthrough.

### Who writes the cleaning schedule required by OSHA in a clinic?

The employer, meaning the medical or dental practice, is responsible for its written cleaning and decontamination schedule under the OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard. A cleaning contractor can work from that schedule and document what it does, but the practice owns the plan. Check the current standard or ask Oregon OSHA if you are unsure what your schedule needs to include.
