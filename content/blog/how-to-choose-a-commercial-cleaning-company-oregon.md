If you are working out how to choose a commercial cleaning company, start by making every bid quote the same scope, frequency and supplies, then verify insurance and Oregon workers' compensation coverage, check references, and read the contract terms before you sign. A walkthrough of your building should come before any number. The rest of this guide shows you how to do each step.

## Start with your own scope of work

The single biggest reason cleaning bids are hard to compare is that each company is pricing a different job. One assumes nightly restrooms, another assumes three nights a week; one includes paper products, another does not. Before you call anyone, write down what you want.

Your scope should list:

- **Areas** — offices, restrooms, break rooms, lobbies, conference rooms, stairwells, warehouse floor.
- **Tasks per area** — trash, restroom cleaning and restocking, vacuuming, mopping, dusting, glass, high-touch disinfection.
- **Frequency per task** — every visit, weekly, monthly, quarterly.
- **Schedule** — which days, and whether cleaning must happen after hours or on weekends.
- **Supplies** — who provides cleaning products, equipment and consumables like paper towels and liners.
- **Exclusions and restrictions** — rooms that are off limits, desks that should not be touched, product requirements.

If you are unsure what to include, our [office cleaning checklist](/blog/office-cleaning-checklist) is a good starting template, and our guide on [how often an office should be cleaned](/blog/how-often-should-an-office-be-cleaned) helps you set frequency.

## Compare bids apples to apples

Once each company has your scope, line the proposals up and check that they actually match it. When one bid looks much lower, the reason is almost always in the scope, not the efficiency.

Look for differences in:

1. **Frequency.** Are restrooms covered on every visit or only some?
2. **Task list.** Does the proposal itemize tasks, or just say "general cleaning"?
3. **Supplies and consumables.** Who buys paper products, soap and liners? Who supplies equipment?
4. **Staffing.** Will the same crew come each time? Who supervises them?
5. **Quality control.** How are inspections done, and how do you report a problem?
6. **Exclusions.** What is not included, and what would be quoted separately?

Ask every company to walk your building before quoting. A provider that quotes sight unseen is guessing at your square footage, traffic and floor types, and the guess usually gets corrected later. A walkthrough is also your first chance to see how they ask questions and whether they notice the things that matter to you.

## Check insurance and Oregon workers' compensation

Cleaning crews work inside your building, often unsupervised, so coverage matters.

### Liability insurance

Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability coverage, and confirm the policy dates are current. Many property managers also ask to be listed as a certificate holder or additional insured; your own insurer or attorney can tell you what your lease or policy requires.

### Workers' compensation

Oregon is strict about this. According to the Oregon Department of Consumer and Business Services, if you have one or more subject workers you are a subject employer and must have Oregon workers' compensation coverage, and nearly every worker in Oregon is a subject worker unless they fall under one of the exceptions in state law ([Oregon DCBS](https://www.oregon.gov/DCBS/wbf/Pages/workers-compensation-coverage.aspx)). A cleaning company with employees should be able to show you proof of coverage.

If you want to confirm it independently, the Oregon Workers' Compensation Division accepts [online requests to identify an employer's insurer](https://wcd.oregon.gov/employer/coverage/Pages/employer-coverage-request.aspx). Be cautious if a company says its cleaners are all independent contractors; DCBS notes that if you have the right to direct and control how someone does the work, that person is probably an employee. This is general information, not legal advice, so check the current rules or talk to a professional about your situation.

### Business registration

You can also look up a company on the [Oregon Secretary of State business registry](https://sos.oregon.gov/business/Pages/find.aspx) to confirm it is registered and active.

## Ask about people, products and process

Insurance tells you what happens when something goes wrong. These questions tell you how likely it is to go wrong in the first place.

- **Background checks and training.** Are crew members background-checked before they enter client buildings? How are they trained?
- **Crew consistency.** Will you get a dedicated crew, or whoever is available that night?
- **Backup coverage.** What happens when a crew member is sick or quits?
- **Supervision.** Does anyone inspect the work on a schedule?
- **Products.** What products do they use? Can they follow your requirements, for example eco-friendly products or facility-specific rules?
- **Safety data.** OSHA's hazard communication standard requires employers who use hazardous chemicals where another employer's workers may be exposed to have a way of giving that employer on-site access to safety data sheets ([29 CFR 1910.1200](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)). Ask how they will share that information with you.
- **Communication.** Do you get a direct line to someone who can act, or a general inbox?

## Check references and read the contract

Ask for references from clients with a similar building type and size, and actually call them. Ask how long they have used the company, how problems are handled, and whether the crew has stayed consistent.

Then read the contract carefully. Look for:

1. A written scope that matches what you were shown at the walkthrough.
2. The schedule, including holidays.
3. Term length and how either side can end the agreement, including notice periods.
4. How price changes are handled if your scope or square footage changes.
5. Key, card and alarm code procedures, and what happens to them if the contract ends.
6. How service complaints are logged and resolved.

If anything in the contract does not match what the salesperson said, ask for it in writing before you sign.

## Red flags to watch for

Knowing how to choose a commercial cleaning company also means knowing when to walk away. Slow down, or step back entirely, if you see:

- A quote given over the phone without a walkthrough.
- No written scope, or a scope that says only "general cleaning."
- Reluctance to provide a certificate of insurance or proof of workers' compensation.
- Every worker described as an independent contractor.
- A different crew every visit with no supervision.
- Pressure to sign quickly, or a long term with no clear exit.
- Claims of services that require a specialist, such as mold remediation or biohazard cleanup, from a company that does not mention any specialist licensing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What questions should I ask a commercial cleaning company?

Ask a commercial cleaning company what exactly is in the scope, how often each task happens, who supplies products and consumables, whether crews are background-checked and trained, whether you get a dedicated crew, how work is inspected, and what happens when a crew member is out. Also ask for a certificate of insurance, proof of workers' compensation and references from similar buildings.

### What insurance should a commercial cleaning company have?

A commercial cleaning company should have general liability insurance and, if it has employees in Oregon, workers' compensation coverage. Oregon DCBS says employers with one or more subject workers must carry workers' compensation. Ask for a current certificate of insurance and proof of coverage, and check with your own insurer about any additional requirements in your lease or policy.

### How can I check if an Oregon cleaning company has workers' comp?

You can check whether an Oregon cleaning company has workers' compensation by asking for proof of coverage and by submitting an online employer coverage request to the Oregon Workers' Compensation Division, which will reply with the employer's insurer information. You can also confirm the business is registered and active through the Oregon Secretary of State business registry.

### Why do commercial cleaning bids vary so much?

Commercial cleaning bids vary mostly because companies are quoting different scopes. Differences in frequency, which tasks are included, who supplies consumables, crew size and supervision all change the price. Give every company the same written scope, insist on a walkthrough, and compare proposals line by line so you are judging the same job.

### Should a cleaning company do a walkthrough before quoting?

Yes, a cleaning company should walk your building before giving a commercial quote. A walkthrough lets the provider see your square footage, floor types, traffic, restrooms and access needs, so the quote reflects the real job. Quotes given without a visit are estimates that often change after service starts, which makes them hard to compare against other bids.
