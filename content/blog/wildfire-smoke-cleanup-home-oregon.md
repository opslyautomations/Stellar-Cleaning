If you are working out how to clean wildfire smoke residue, the short version is this: change your HVAC filter first, then clean every hard surface wet rather than dry, working from the top of the room down, and wash every fabric you can. A light film and fine ash after a smoky stretch is a cleaning job. Heavy soot, a burned smell that lives in the walls, or any actual fire damage is a job for a fire restoration specialist.

## What wildfire smoke leaves behind indoors

Even with the windows shut, smoke gets in through door gaps, vents and every trip in from outside. It leaves very fine particle residue. You see it as a gray film on window sills and dark surfaces, a gritty feel on countertops, and dust that comes back a day after you wipe it. You smell it in curtains, couch cushions and carpet.

In Bend, Redmond and Prineville, that residue mixes with the high-desert dust already in every house. In Eugene, homes sealed up for days during a smoke event tend to hold the smell because the air has had nowhere to go.

Knowing how to clean wildfire smoke residue mostly comes down to one goal: get the particles out of the air, then off the surfaces, without throwing them back into the air along the way.

## Start with the air: filters and HVAC

Clean the air before you clean the surfaces, or the surfaces will just collect the residue again.

- **Replace the HVAC filter.** The EPA recommends a MERV 13 filter, or the highest rating your system can handle, and says to check filters frequently and change them when they look dirty. After a heavy smoke event, a filter can load up much faster than its usual replacement interval. See the EPA page on [wildfires and indoor air quality](https://www.epa.gov/emergencies-iaq/wildfires-and-indoor-air-quality-iaq).
- **Run a portable air cleaner if you have one.** HEPA air cleaners help pull fine particles out of the room. Match the unit to the room size.
- **No air cleaner?** OSU Extension shares instructions for a do-it-yourself box fan filter, which can be an effective option when other air cleaners are not available. Their [wildfire air quality resources](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/announcements/air-quality-resources-during-wildfires) walk through it.
- **Do not buy a duct cleaning on reflex.** EPA guidance is that air ducts only need cleaning as needed, such as when you see excessive dust buildup or particles coming out of the supply registers.

One thing to skip is an ozone generator. The EPA warns that ozone can damage the lungs and that no federal agency has approved these devices for use in occupied spaces. Read the EPA's position on [ozone generators sold as air cleaners](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/ozone-generators-are-sold-air-cleaners) before anyone offers to run one in your house.

## How to clean wildfire smoke residue from hard surfaces

Oregon's guidance, published by DEQ with the Oregon Health Authority and Oregon OSHA, is clear on method: use a damp cloth on indoor surfaces, use a wet mop on floors, and do not vacuum ash unless the vacuum has a HEPA filter. The same [Oregon DEQ guidance on returning home after a wildfire](https://www.oregon.gov/deq/wildfires/Pages/Home-that-Survived-a-Wildfire.aspx) says to avoid dry sweeping and leaf blowers, to keep children away from areas with visible ash or dust until they are cleaned, and that an N95 respirator, worn properly, offers protection where a cloth mask does not.

Here is the order that works:

1. **Ventilate once the outdoor air is clean.** Check current air quality before you open up. Cleaning with smoky air still coming in is wasted effort.
2. **Put on gloves.** Rubber gloves for the wiping, and an N95 if you are disturbing visible ash.
3. **Work top to bottom.** Ceiling fan blades, tops of door frames, shelves and light fixtures first, then counters and furniture, then baseboards, then floors. Anything you knock loose falls onto surfaces you have not cleaned yet.
4. **Use damp microfiber, not a dry duster.** Dry dusting puts fine particles straight back in the air. Rinse or swap cloths often so you are not smearing residue around.
5. **Do the window side of the house carefully.** Sills, tracks and blinds catch the most residue because that is where the air leaked in.
6. **Wash hard floors with a wet mop.** If there is grit on the floor, mist it lightly before you sweep, then mop.
7. **Vacuum only with a HEPA vacuum.** That goes for carpet, rugs and upholstery.
8. **Clean inside cabinets that were open or vented.** Closed cabinets are usually fine. Open shelving and pantry fronts are not.

## Soft goods: bedding, curtains, upholstery and carpet

Fabric holds smell long after hard surfaces come clean.

- Wash bedding, throws, pillow covers and curtains that can go in the machine. More than one wash is sometimes needed.
- Take dry-clean-only items to a dry cleaner and tell them it is smoke odor.
- HEPA-vacuum upholstered furniture, mattresses and any fabric you cannot wash, including the underside of cushions.
- Vacuum carpet slowly in overlapping passes with a HEPA vacuum. If the carpet still smells after thorough vacuuming, it may need hot-water extraction from a carpet cleaning company, which is not a service we provide.

## When it is not a cleaning job anymore

Be honest with yourself about where cleaning stops. A cleaning crew handles residue on surfaces that are otherwise sound. A fire restoration specialist handles damage. Call a restoration company, and your insurance carrier, if you see any of these:

- Black, oily or greasy soot that smears when you wipe it
- A smoke smell that comes from inside walls, ceilings, insulation or the duct system
- Ash or debris that came in through a broken window, damaged roof or vent
- Any charring, heat damage or melted materials
- A home or building that was inside or right next to a burn area

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I vacuum wildfire ash with a regular vacuum?

No, use a vacuum with a HEPA filter or do not vacuum ash at all. Oregon DEQ guidance, issued with the Oregon Health Authority, says a regular vacuum can blow fine ash back into the air. Without a HEPA vacuum, use a damp cloth on surfaces and a wet mop on hard floors instead. Avoid dry sweeping and leaf blowers for the same reason.

### What is the best HVAC filter for wildfire smoke?

The EPA recommends a MERV 13 filter, or the highest-rated filter your system is designed to handle. Higher-rated filters capture more fine smoke particles, but some older systems are not built for them, so check your system's manual or ask an HVAC technician. During and after a smoke event, check the filter often and replace it when it looks dirty.

### Should I get my air ducts cleaned after wildfire smoke?

Usually not, unless you see a specific problem. EPA guidance is that ducts should be cleaned only as needed, for example when there is excessive dust buildup inside them or particles are visibly coming out of the supply registers. Replacing the filter and cleaning surfaces fixes most smoke residue. If the smoke smell seems to come from the duct system itself, have an HVAC professional inspect it.

### Do ozone generators safely remove smoke smell?

No, the EPA advises against using ozone generators in occupied spaces. Ozone can damage the lungs and cause coughing, chest pain and throat irritation, and no federal agency has approved these devices for use where people are present. Washing fabrics, cleaning surfaces, fresh filters and fresh air are the safer route. Persistent smoke odor in the structure is a job for a restoration specialist.
