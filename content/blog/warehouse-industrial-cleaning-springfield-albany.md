Good warehouse cleaning services keep four things under control: dust, floors, break rooms and restrooms, all scheduled around your shifts rather than in the way of them. OSHA expects workplaces to be kept clean, orderly and sanitary, so a cleaning plan is part of how a warehouse stays safe as well as how it looks. Here is what to include, what to leave to specialists, and how to time it.

## What warehouse cleaning services should cover

Warehouses and light industrial buildings combine very different spaces under one roof. A useful scope treats each one on its own terms:

- **Warehouse floor and aisles.** Sweeping and dust control, spill response routines, and floor care matched to sealed concrete or coated surfaces.
- **Docks and entries.** Grit, mud and debris tracked in from trucks and yards, plus mats that need regular attention.
- **Break rooms.** Tables, counters, sinks, microwaves, refrigerator exteriors and trash, in rooms that get used hard every shift.
- **Restrooms and locker rooms.** Sanitation and restocking sized to the headcount on each shift.
- **Offices and front areas.** Desks, floors, glass and lobby surfaces, usually cleaned to an office standard.
- **Trash and recycling.** Emptying, liner replacement, and sorting to match your recycling program.

The mix depends on how the building is used. A distribution center with heavy dock traffic needs different attention from a quiet storage building with a small office.

## OSHA housekeeping rules every warehouse should know

Federal OSHA's walking-working surfaces standard, [29 CFR 1910.22](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22), opens with housekeeping. It requires that all places of employment, passageways, storerooms, service rooms and walking-working surfaces be kept in a clean, orderly and sanitary condition. Workroom floors must be kept clean and, as far as feasible, dry. Walking-working surfaces must be kept free of hazards such as sharp or protruding objects, loose boards, corrosion, leaks, spills, snow and ice.

Storage areas have their own rule. Under 29 CFR 1910.176(c), storage areas must be kept free from accumulations of materials that create tripping, fire, explosion or pest harborage hazards.

In Oregon, workplace safety is enforced by Oregon OSHA, which adopted the federal walking-working surfaces rules by reference in its [Division 2, Subdivision D rules](https://osha.oregon.gov/rules/final/division-2/Pages/div2-subdivision-D.aspx), effective November 1, 2017.

Two points are worth keeping in mind:

1. These duties belong to the employer. A cleaning contract helps you meet them, but it does not transfer them.
2. Housekeeping is continuous. A nightly clean handles the build-up, but spills and debris during a shift still need a quick response from your own team.

This is a summary, not legal advice. Check the current rules or talk to a safety professional about your facility.

## Dust control, and when dust is a specialist job

Most warehouse dust is ordinary: grit from docks, cardboard fiber, tire dust from forklifts and pallet jacks, and whatever comes in on the wind. In Prineville and the rest of Central Oregon, dry conditions add fine dust to the mix. Regular dust control keeps it off products, equipment, vents and workstations, and keeps floors from getting slick.

Some dust is a different matter. OSHA warns that many materials can form [combustible dust](https://www.osha.gov/combustible-dust), including food products such as sugar, flour and starch, grain, wood, paper, plastics, rubber, metals and pharmaceuticals. In the right conditions, accumulated combustible dust can fuel a fire or explosion.

If your facility handles materials like these, dust control is a safety engineering question, not a routine cleaning task. It calls for a hazard assessment, the right equipment and methods, and people trained for that environment. A general cleaning crew should not be your combustible dust program. If you are unsure whether it applies to you, Oregon OSHA offers free, confidential [consultation services](https://osha.oregon.gov/consult) that can help assess hazards without issuing citations.

## Floors, break rooms and restrooms around shifts

Warehouses rarely close at five. Many run early, late or overnight shifts, and cleaning has to fit around the gaps.

### Floors

High-traffic concrete takes a beating from forklifts, carts and boots. Regular sweeping and mopping or buffing appropriate to the surface keep grit from grinding into coatings and keep aisles from getting slippery. Entry and dock areas need the most attention, especially through the wet months in the Willamette Valley.

### Break rooms

A break room shared by every shift needs cleaning between peaks, not once a day at a random time. Counters, tables, sinks, microwave interiors where agreed, and refrigerator handles are the high-touch points. A clean break room is one of the few perks every employee notices.

### Restrooms

Restroom supplies tend to run low around shift changes. Restocking and cleaning just ahead of the busiest changeover means the next shift starts with full dispensers.

A sample shift-aware routine:

1. Before the morning shift, clean and restock restrooms and break rooms
2. Between shifts, check restrooms, empty break-room trash and wipe tables
3. After the last shift, clean floors in offices and common areas, take out trash and recycling, and wipe high-touch points
4. Weekly, detail dock entries, vents, ledges within safe reach and locker rooms

## What to leave to specialists

Some warehouse jobs need equipment, training or licensing beyond a cleaning contract:

- Combustible dust collection systems and hazard assessments
- High-level cleaning of racking tops, roof structure and lights that requires lifts and fall protection
- Spills of hazardous chemicals, which should follow your own spill response plan and safety data sheets
- Industrial equipment cleaning that requires lockout or machine-specific procedures
- Pest control

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should a warehouse be cleaned?

Most warehouses need restrooms and break rooms cleaned daily, plus regular floor and dust work on a weekly cycle. Facilities with multiple shifts often need restrooms checked and restocked between shifts as well. The right frequency depends on headcount, dock traffic, the materials handled and the size of the building, so it is best set during a walkthrough.

### What does OSHA require for warehouse housekeeping?

OSHA requires workplaces, passageways, storerooms and walking-working surfaces to be kept clean, orderly and sanitary, with floors kept clean and as dry as feasible. Storage areas must be kept free of accumulations that create tripping, fire, explosion or pest hazards. In Oregon, Oregon OSHA enforces these rules. Check the current standard or ask a safety professional for specifics.

### Can a cleaning company handle combustible dust?

A general cleaning company should not be your combustible dust program. Combustible dust from materials such as flour, sugar, wood, plastics or metals can fuel fires and explosions, and controlling it requires a hazard assessment, suitable equipment and trained people. Oregon OSHA offers free, confidential consultations that can help you work out whether combustible dust applies to your facility.

### Can warehouse cleaning happen during operating hours?

Yes, some warehouse cleaning can happen during operating hours, such as restroom checks, break-room resets and trash runs between shifts. Floor work in active aisles is usually better done when forklift and foot traffic is lowest, often overnight or early morning. A good schedule follows your shift pattern instead of forcing operations around the cleaning crew.

### Who is responsible for warehouse safety if we hire a cleaning company?

The employer remains responsible for meeting OSHA housekeeping requirements in its own workplace, even with a cleaning contract in place. The cleaning company is responsible for its own crew and for doing the work in its scope. Your team still needs to respond to spills and hazards that come up during shifts.
