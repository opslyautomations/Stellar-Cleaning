A useful restaurant cleaning checklist for Oregon owners splits the work three ways: your trained kitchen staff clean and sanitize food-contact surfaces during service, a contract cleaning crew handles floors, restrooms, dining rooms and other facility surfaces after close, and specialists handle hood and grease duct cleaning. Inspectors look at the result, not who did it, so every surface needs an owner.

## What Oregon restaurant inspections look at

Oregon's restaurant rules are set by the Oregon Health Authority. Its [Food Sanitation Rules](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/healthyenvironments/foodsafety/Pages/index.aspx) were updated to adopt the 2022 FDA Food Code with Oregon-specific amendments, effective January 1, 2026. The same update phases in a certified food protection manager requirement, with the first step in 2029 and the second in 2031.

State law, [ORS 624.060](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors624.html), calls for every restaurant to be inspected at least once every six months. In practice, routine inspections are carried out by your county's environmental health office, which is also who to call with licensing questions. Inspection reports are published on a statewide online portal that OHA links from its food safety page.

Most inspection findings fall into food handling (temperatures, hand washing, cross-contamination) rather than general cleaning. Cleaning still shows up, though, in a few predictable places:

- Food-contact surfaces and utensils that are not cleaned and sanitized often enough
- Non-food-contact surfaces with built-up grease, food debris or residue
- Floors, walls and ceilings in kitchen and storage areas that are not kept clean
- Restrooms and hand sinks that are dirty or not stocked
- Chemical storage too close to food
- Garbage areas that attract pests

This is a practical summary, not legal advice. Check the current rules or ask your county environmental health office about anything specific to your operation.

## Who cleans what in a restaurant

The Food Code expects food-contact surfaces in continuous use to be cleaned at least every four hours, and whenever there is a change between raw animal foods and ready-to-eat foods. That timing means sanitizing food-contact surfaces is a job for your kitchen staff during the shift. A night crew cannot do it for you.

The Food Code also says physical facilities should be cleaned as often as necessary to keep them clean, and during periods when the least amount of food is exposed, such as after closing. That is where a contract cleaner fits.

A sensible split looks like this:

- **Kitchen staff:** prep tables, cutting boards, slicers, utensils, food storage containers, dish machines, sanitizer buckets, and anything food touches.
- **Contract cleaning crew:** dining room, restrooms, entry and host stand, floors front and back, walls, baseboards, non-food-contact surfaces, break areas and offices.
- **Specialists:** kitchen exhaust hoods, grease ducts and fans, grease trap pumping, and pest control.

Write the split down. Restaurants that lose points on cleaning are often ones where each side assumed the other had it.

## Restaurant cleaning checklist for the front of house

Guests see the front of house, so it carries your reputation as much as your inspection result. A nightly front-of-house list usually covers:

1. Tables, chairs and booth seats wiped, including chair legs and table bases
2. High chairs and booster seats cleaned
3. Menus, condiment caddies and check presenters wiped
4. Host stand, POS screens (exteriors only) and door handles disinfected
5. Entry glass and interior windows cleaned of fingerprints
6. Restrooms cleaned and disinfected, with soap, towels and paper restocked
7. Floors swept and mopped, with entry mats cleaned
8. Trash and recycling emptied and liners replaced

Weekly, add baseboards, light fixtures, vents, wall scuffs, window sills and the undersides of tables where gum and grime collect.

## Back-of-house restaurant cleaning checklist

Kitchen floors and facility surfaces are where a contract crew earns its keep, because a closing line cook rarely has the time or energy to do them properly.

Nightly, after food is put away:

1. Floors under and behind equipment swept, degreased and mopped, with floor drains cleared of debris
2. Walls and splash zones near cook lines wiped of grease
3. Exteriors of reach-ins, shelving and equipment wiped
4. Hand sinks cleaned and restocked
5. Mop sinks and janitorial areas left clean and orderly
6. Trash and recycling taken out and bins cleaned

Weekly or on a set cycle:

- Walk-in floors and the outside of shelving units
- Ceiling tiles and vents near cooking areas
- Dry storage floors and shelving edges
- Employee restrooms and break areas given a deeper clean

Keep cleaning chemicals stored away from and below food, labeled, and in their own area. Any crew working in your kitchen should follow that rule too, and should use only products you have approved for a food-service space.

## Hood and grease duct cleaning is specialist work

Kitchen exhaust hoods, ducts and fans collect grease that is a fire hazard. Cleaning them is governed by fire code, with NFPA 96 as the standard fire officials commonly reference, and it is done by specialist hood-cleaning contractors. Frequency depends on what and how much you cook, and your local fire marshal is the authority on what applies to you.

A general cleaning crew can wipe the outside of a hood and keep the area around it clean. It should not take apart a hood system, and you should not hire a general cleaner who offers to. The same goes for grease traps and pest control.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often are restaurants inspected in Oregon?

Oregon law calls for restaurants to be inspected at least once every six months. In most of the state, the inspections are carried out by the county environmental health office, which also handles licensing questions and complaints. Inspectors can also return for follow-up visits when a violation needs to be corrected, so it pays to keep cleaning consistent rather than preparing only for routine visits.

### Can a cleaning company sanitize food prep surfaces?

Sanitizing food-contact surfaces is best kept with your trained kitchen staff, because the Food Code expects it throughout service, at least every four hours for surfaces in continuous use and whenever tasks change. A contract crew working after close can clean floors, walls, restrooms and non-food-contact surfaces, and should only use products you have approved for a food-service space.

### Who should clean a restaurant kitchen hood?

A specialist hood-cleaning contractor should clean kitchen exhaust hoods, ducts and fans. The work is governed by fire code, with NFPA 96 commonly referenced, and the required frequency depends on the type and volume of cooking. A general cleaning crew can wipe hood exteriors and the surrounding area, but should not disassemble or service the exhaust system.

### Where can I see a restaurant's inspection report in Oregon?

Inspection reports for licensed food facilities in Oregon are published on a statewide online portal linked from the Oregon Health Authority's food safety page. Reports appear after a short delay rather than on the day of inspection. Your county environmental health office can also answer questions about a specific report.

### What should be cleaned every night in a restaurant?

Every night, clean dining tables and seating, restrooms, door handles, host stand surfaces, and all floors front and back, including under equipment and around drains. Wipe grease from walls and equipment exteriors, clean hand sinks and restock them, and take out trash. Food-contact surfaces should already have been cleaned and sanitized by staff during service.
